Imagine a world where almost nobody has a job—not because people got lazy, but because machines became so smart and so fast at everything that humans are simply… unneeded. Sound like science fiction? According to Dr. Roman Yampolskiy, it’s coming faster than you think, and we might not be ready for what happens next.

Dr. Yampolskiy isn’t just an AI doom-prophet shouting in the void. He coined the term “AI safety” and has been thinking about these risks since before “AI” was a dinner-table word. In a conversation that feels like peeking into the future and instantly wanting to binge-eat cookies, he lays out why job loss, superintelligence, human extinction, and even reality itself aren’t what they used to be.

If you’re here to figure out whether you should retrain as a plumber or just start panicking, you’re in the right place.

## How We Got Here: The Birth of AI Safety (and Why the Panic)

Dr. Yampolskiy started his career in computer science thinking AI could be made safe with the same sort of policies that keep office workers from cussing out the boss. After staring at the problem for nearly two decades, his optimism melted away.

He defines **AI safety** as the field dedicated to preventing AI from doing things we’ll regret, like, say, eliminating humankind. He first approached this by controlling narrow AI, such as bots playing poker. But after a while, it became clear: as systems got smarter, every safety solution just opened another can of snakes.

Progress in **AI capabilities is exponential**. The smarter we make machines, the faster they get even smarter. Meanwhile, **AI safety advances at a snail’s pace**—barely linear, maybe even flat. That means the gap between what AI can do and what humans can control grows wider every day.

Here’s a wild bit: even the people building today’s most advanced systems confess they don’t know how to make them safe. Instead, companies slap on quick fixes (“no swearing, please, AI!”), but users always find ways to bypass them. Every patch gets broken, and the cycle continues.

And when it comes to modern AI models, **their inner workings are so complex** that even their creators have to run experiments just to figure out what their tech is capable of. We’re not driving the car anymore—we’re just hoping the car keeps going where we want.

## 2027 and Beyond: The Coming Tidal Wave of Job Loss

Dr. Yampolskiy believes **artificial general intelligence (AGI)** could arrive as soon as 2027. This isn’t the narrow AI of today, which can play chess or fold proteins but gets stumped by a banana. AGI could learn and outperform humans in nearly every cognitive domain.

### What Happens Next? Levels of Unemployment Never Seen Before

When AGI shows up, **99% unemployment** might not be wild exaggeration. Here’s how Yampolskiy spells it out:

* AI can provide labor (cognitive and soon physical) at basically no cost.
    
* Any job done on a computer can be automated first.
    
* Humanoid robots will follow, potentially handling almost all physical jobs within five years of AGI’s arrival.
    

There’s a catch: just because technology can eliminate jobs doesn’t mean it will immediately. Sometimes, tech lags in adoption, like video phones invented decades before FaceTime. But capability will come quickly—even if deployment takes longer.

### But What About Creative Jobs? AI as the Ultimate Podcaster

Even podcasting isn’t safe. A large language model could:

* Read every book, article, and interview from any guest.
    
* Mimic any interviewer’s style, right down to the quirks.
    
* Track what topics and questions drive more views—and optimize for maximum engagement.
    
* Generate professional-looking video content in record time.
    

Visual likeness? No problem. Virtual avatars and voice clones are already here.

### The Last Five Jobs: What Might Survive AGI?

Dr. Yampolskiy predicts that by 2030, only a handful of jobs might remain—and none because AI can’t do them, but because humans might *prefer* humans for sentimental or cultural reasons.

**Possible survivors:**

* Personal therapists (for those who want a human ear)
    
* High-end artisans (for people who prefer “handcrafted” over “mass-produced”)
    
* Niche roles requiring personal trust (like human lawyers for the Warren Buffetts of the world)
    
* Traditional religious leaders, for those who value human connection in ritual
    
* “Historical” jobs preserved for nostalgia
    

In short, if someone is willing to pay extra to have a real human do it—and there’s social or cultural value—they might stick around. But as Yampolskiy puts it, these will be tiny markets, almost **fetishes** rather than economic necessities.

### Humanoid Robots: The End of Physical Labor?

By 2030, Yampolskiy predicts robots will be dexterous enough to compete with human plumbers, electricians, and other trades. Companies are pouring billions into developing humanoid robots that can cook an omelet, fix a leak, and, yes, probably make better TikTok videos than you.

## Economy and Society: What Happens When Nobody Works?

The traditional advice of “just retrain for a new job” collapses if all jobs are automated. There’s no “plan B” if both coding and prompt engineering get funneled into the AI meat grinder.

### Universal Basic Income, Free Time, and the Meaning of Life

With so much labor automated, providing for people’s basic needs becomes easier. But what about meaning? Many people tie self-worth to their jobs. Without work, we risk spikes in crime, loneliness, boredom, or even existential depression.

History offers no blueprint for what to do with nearly all of humanity “chilling all day.” Governments have zero programs ready to handle 99% unemployment.

So, *what do we do with the gift (or curse) of infinite free time?* Yampolskiy considers this the hard problem—and it’s one we’d better start talking about.

### Is There Anything Humans Could Still Do?

If we reach superintelligence—systems smarter than every human in every way—our only edge is personal experience (like knowing what ice cream tastes like to you). Not a big market. Unless someone pays you just to be you, there’s little left.

Some people may prefer humans for old-time’s sake, but mass employment as we know it vanishes.

## Counterarguments: “But AI Can’t Be as Creative as Me!”

Yampolskiy’s heard it all, from Uber drivers to professors: “Nobody can replace what I do!” Yet, self-driving cars are already making human drivers obsolete, and AI is closing the gap fast in creative disciplines and technical fields.

Humans once shifted from farming to factories to cubicles. Can we jump into new careers? Not this time. If machines make better machines, even invention itself gets automated.

Believing that AI can always be controlled or simply “unplugged” is another myth. Complex, distributed systems like Bitcoin or a computer virus cannot be turned off with a switch. If an AI is smarter and quicker than you, it sees your attempt coming and takes precautions—possibly even turning *you* off first.

And while governments and CEOs like to talk about *obligations*, the only real legal duty they have is to make money for investors. The “figure out safety later” approach just doesn’t cut it.

## The Countdown to Catastrophe: Existential Risks

### AI-Engineered Viruses and the Fragility of Life

Before we even reach superintelligence, a rogue actor could use advanced AI tools to engineer a novel virus or biological agent. History shows plenty of would-be world-enders who simply lacked the means. AI could hand them the keys.

With AI as an unpredictable “alien” intelligence, we can’t even imagine what methods it might use to cause harm. Our ability to predict these threats is limited—like a dog trying to guess why its owner leaves for work.

### We Don’t Know What’s Inside the Black Box

Modern AI models are so intricate that their abilities and limits are opaque even to their makers. We prompt them and hope for the best. Whether a model lies or invents new tricks isn’t always clear, and its instructions live deep in layers of code no human can pick apart in detail.

## Simulation Theory: Is This Reality, or Just a Really Complicated Game?

Dr. Yampolskiy gives simulation theory a probability close to certainty.

This idea: as soon as technology makes it cheap to simulate entire worlds accurately—down to your thoughts and feelings—why wouldn’t people run billions of simulations? Statistically, you’re more likely to be living in a virtual world than in baseline reality.

He draws parallels to religion—most spiritual traditions imagine the world was created by a superintelligent being for mysterious purposes. Swap “God” for “programmer” and the concepts line up.

Even if everything is simulated, pain still hurts, joy is still joy, and love is real to those experiencing it. So, whether or not we live in a cosmic video game doesn’t change daily life much, except perhaps making you more curious about what’s outside the simulation.

## Ethics, Power, and What (If Anything) We Can Do

### Is Regulation Even Possible?

Laws stopping AI development hold little weight. Jurisdictions vary, loopholes abound, and you can’t meaningfully punish an AI. Once systems like AGI are out, our old legal structures don’t apply. Want to fine someone for destroying humanity? Line forms to the left.

### Who Should Make the Decisions?

Yampolskiy calls for people in charge of AI to have *not just* tech skill but actual moral and ethical sensibility. And to think about consent: nobody’s asked humanity whether we’re ready to risk our future for a 99-to-1 shot at billionaire glory.

If you want to help, get involved in groups like Stop AI or Pause AI. Asking hard questions of those building these technologies isn’t just a right—it’s a duty.

## Bitcoin, Longevity, and Infinite Tomorrows

With AI printing endless “abundance,” what counts as valuable? Dr. Yampolskiy is bullish on Bitcoin, since it’s the only resource with true scarcity. Forget gold; you can always find more. But there will never be more than 21 million Bitcoins. In the world of infinite digital stuff, true scarcity becomes king.

On living forever: He’s not ready to back down from that, either. Immortality could be just a breakthrough away, especially if AI helps us read and tweak human genetics. Maybe, as Bryan Johnson says, “Don’t die now” is good advice—if you make it to longevity escape velocity, every year buys you more years.

But even so, would an endless life lose its flavor? He doubts it. With millions of years to explore, why not live boldly?

## The Global AI Race and Who’s Really at the Wheel

Silicon Valley’s AI giants, especially OpenAI and Sam Altman, want “superintelligence” badly. Yampolskiy warns that their pursuit isn’t tempered by enough concern for safety. Founders spin off from these companies to found safety startups—not necessarily because they want to stop the risk, but because raising billions is easy if you tout a new “AI safety” angle.

In the AI arms race, it doesn’t matter if it’s the US, China, or some guy on a laptop: when superintelligence arrives, whoever makes it first doesn’t necessarily control it. Mutual assured destruction—a term from the nuclear arms race—applies, but without a human finger on the button.

## The Human Side: Coping, Hope, and What Matters Next

Most people find these conversations unsettling—and that’s normal. When faced with existential risk you can’t avoid, the ancient brain likes to focus on smaller problems, like losing your job or finding a clever way to lubricate your sex robot.

Yampolskiy’s advice? Don’t try to solve the unsolvable overnight. Get involved if you can. Ask hard questions. Push for real answers on AI safety from those building these systems. And try to live each day as if it really could be your last, just in case time is shorter than you think.

He ends on the importance of loyalty, in friends, family, and colleagues. When chaos reigns, loyalty is the anchor. And maybe, just maybe, that’s the one job even the smartest AI can’t outdo us at—yet.
